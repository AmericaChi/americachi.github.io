Computational Biophysics of Biomolecular Interactions (CBBI)

Decoding and designing biomolecular interactions

L. América Chi, PhD

My research seeks to predict how biomolecules recognize each other, how mutations rewire that recognition, and how to design molecules that exploit it, by combining physics-based simulations with machine learning.

On this page: Background · 1. Mechanisms · 2. Prediction · 3. Design

Background: one mutation can change everything, and we cannot test them all

A single amino-acid change can let a virus escape an antibody or a host restriction factor, shift the color a photoreceptor detects, or alter how a drug binds its target. The number of possible variants is enormous, and experiments can only test a small fraction of them. Physics-based simulations reveal why a mutation matters, but are expensive; machine learning is fast, but needs good data and often lacks a physical explanation.

I combine both: molecular simulations to understand mechanisms, and data-driven models to scale those insights to thousands of variants and candidate molecules.

I approach this problem at three levels:

1. Uncover the molecular mechanisms through which mutations change interactions

(What happens, atom by atom, when a mutation changes how two molecules interact?)

Using molecular dynamics, enhanced sampling, and structural modeling, I study how mutations reshape interfaces and conformational ensembles. With experimental collaborators, I built AI-based structural models of the interface between the antiviral protein MxA and the Thogotovirus nucleoprotein, identifying mutations that drive restriction or viral escape, later confirmed in viral mini-replicon assays (bioRxiv 2026). I have also studied the molecular basis of the spectral shift in zebrafish cone opsins (bioRxiv 2024) and anion selectivity and fluoride transport in CLC transporters (Springer 2022).

2. Predict the energetic impact of mutations with physics + machine learning

(Can we predict, quickly and accurately, which mutations matter?)

Fast empirical tools such as FoldX can scan thousands of mutations, but their errors limit their use. I showed that combining molecular dynamics with FoldX can predict SARS-CoV-2 antibody-escape mutations, benchmarked against large-scale experimental data (Scientific Reports 2024). Building on this, we trained neural networks that correct FoldX free-energy estimates, substantially improving predictions for single and multiple mutations (ACS Omega 2026). Because these models are only as good as their data, I developed PepXPro, a framework to curate, generate, and optimize structure–affinity datasets of protein–peptide complexes (bioRxiv 2026).

3. Design peptides and small molecules that exploit these interactions

(Can we use this understanding to design new therapeutics?)

I apply these tools to molecular design: de novo peptides targeting resistin (J. Mol. Model. 2020); protecting the bioactive peptide Ang-(1-7) with PAMAM dendrimer nanocarriers (J. Comput.-Aided Mol. Des. 2022) and understanding its oligomerization (J. Chem. Inf. Model. 2023); and virtual screening of α5β1-integrin ligands, which supported a provisional patent filing (2025). Currently, I design antimalarial PROTACs and map the ubiquitin–proteasome system of Plasmodium falciparum in a U.S. Department of Defense–funded project, and search for antifungal compounds against potato pathogens.

Toolbox

Molecular dynamics (GROMACS) · enhanced sampling (metadynamics, replica exchange, PLUMED) · binding free energies · docking and virtual screening · AlphaFold and structural modeling · neural networks and ML · Python · HPC (CPU/GPU)
